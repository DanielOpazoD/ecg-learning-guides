## Cardiac masses and thrombus

Left atrial myxoma, LV thrombus and papillary fibroelastoma

A cardiac mass is described by its location, attachment, size, shape and mobility. The place of the mass, the wall motion beside it and an enhancing agent help to tell a thrombus from a tumor and from a normal structure.

**Left atrial myxoma prolapsing into the LV.** A 2.7 × 1.9 cm mass in the LA is attached by a stalk to the atrial septum at the fossa ovalis[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). In diastole it prolapses 0.6 cm past the mitral annulus into the LV, and in systole it returns to the LA. The mean gradient across the mitral valve is 1.6 mmHg at 70/min.

### Describing a mass

The report of a cardiac mass should give its location, attachment, size, echogenicity, shape and mobility, and a differential diagnosis such as a tumor, a vegetation or a thrombus[36](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-36). A pedunculated mass is attached by a thin stalk that lets it move; a sessile mass is attached by a broad base that prevents significant movement[36](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-36). In adults the two most common primary tumors, the myxoma and the papillary fibroelastoma, often present with stroke or another embolism[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

### Normal structures that mimic a mass

Normal structures can be taken for a mass on transthoracic echocardiography[45](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-45), and the report should name a normal variant as such[36](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-36):

- **Right atrium.** A prominent Eustachian valve and a Chiari network[36](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-36), [45](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-45).
- **Atrial septum.** Lipomatous hypertrophy[45](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-45): thickening that spares the fossa ovalis and gives the septum a dumbbell shape[36](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-36). A myxoma, in contrast, is usually attached at the fossa ovalis.
- **Valves.** Strands, or Lambl's excrescences: filiform structures no wider than 2 mm at the line of closure, on the atrial side of the mitral valve and the ventricular side of the aortic valve[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

### Left atrial myxoma

A myxoma lies in the LA in more than 75% of cases, attached by a stalk to the fossa ovalis in more than 90%. Myxomas are gelatinous and pedunculated[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). A large myxoma can obstruct the mitral orifice and cause mitral stenosis; a mobile, villous or friable one has a high risk of embolism. Up to one third of patients have evidence of embolism, and surgery is generally indicated once the diagnosis is made[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

In the figure, a tumor longer than the distance from its stalk to the mitral annulus reaches into the LV in diastole and returns to the LA in systole. The mean gradient is the mean of 4V² over diastole on the CW inflow trace, with the stroke volume held, at a heart rate of 70/min; [chapter 14](https://intro2echo.com/ms) gives the bands of the mean gradient in rheumatic mitral stenosis.

### LV thrombus

An LV thrombus forms where the wall motion is abnormal: after an acute myocardial infarction, and in a dilated LV with poor function. After an anterior infarction it is more common than after an infarction elsewhere (11.5% against 2.3%), and more common again with an ejection fraction below 40% (17.8%); most thrombi (90%) form within 14 days of the infarction[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). [Chapter 10](https://intro2echo.com/segments) shows the segments of each coronary territory.

A thrombus is a discrete mass with well-defined margins, distinct from the endocardium, seen in systole and diastole in an area of abnormal wall motion. It should be seen in two orthogonal views, an apical and a short-axis view[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). There are three types[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44):

- **Mural.** Flat and parallel to the endocardium, with one surface facing the blood.
- **Protruding.** Standing into the cavity, with more than one surface facing the blood.
- **Mobile.** Moving on its own, in part or as a whole.

Embolism is least common with a mural thrombus and most common with a mobile one; yet 40% of embolic events occur in patients whose thrombi are neither protruding nor mobile[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). In the figure a thrombus is called protruding when its thickness is more than 0.35 of the half-length of its base.

Near-field clutter, false tendons, trabeculations and a foreshortened apex can mimic a thrombus[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44); clutter does not move with the wall and appears to pass through it[46](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-46). A focal zone at the apex, a higher frequency and color Doppler at a low aliasing velocity help; when doubt remains, an enhancing agent should be used. TTE is better than TEE for an apical thrombus, far from the transesophageal probe[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

### Papillary fibroelastoma

About 80% of papillary fibroelastomas lie on the valves, most often the aortic valve and then the mitral valve. On a valve the tumor lies typically on the downstream side: the aortic side of the aortic valve and the ventricular side of the mitral valve, the opposite of the usual site of an endocarditis lesion. It rarely causes significant valve dysfunction[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

The tumor is homogeneous and often mobile, with a dense central stalk and frond-like extensions, like a sea anemone. Most are smaller than 20 mm (mean 8–9 mm). TTE detects about 62% of them and TEE about 77%[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). In published cases, the mobility of the tumor was the only independent predictor of death or nonfatal embolism[47](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-47). The ASE notes that patients with symptoms, or with a large or very mobile tumor, should undergo surgery, and that a small sessile tumor in a patient without symptoms may be watched[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

### Enhancing agents, TEE and CMR

An ultrasound enhancing agent fills the cavities, and a thrombus then shows as a filling defect; the ASE recommends an agent when an LV thrombus cannot be ruled in or out without one[48](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-48). The uptake of the agent in a mass shows its blood supply: a thrombus and a papillary fibroelastoma are generally avascular and do not enhance, and a myxoma has a poor blood supply and enhances in part[48](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-48). Complete enhancement, or enhancement greater than that of the myocardium, supports a highly vascular tumor, most often malignant. An avascular mass can appear to enhance in part in the far field, so the uptake is judged with the mass in the near field[48](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-48).

In patients at high risk, against delayed-enhancement CMR, echocardiography detected 33% of LV thrombi without contrast and 61% with contrast; the thrombi that contrast echocardiography missed were more likely mural, or small at the apex[49](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-49). CMR has the highest sensitivity and specificity for an LV thrombus, and it should be considered when contrast echocardiography finds none but the suspicion persists[48](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-48). TEE may be better than TTE for a cardiac tumor, especially a myxoma or a papillary fibroelastoma[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44).

### Key points

1. A left atrial myxoma is usually attached by a stalk to the fossa ovalis. A large one prolapses into the LV in diastole and can obstruct the mitral valve.
2. An LV thrombus lies in a segment with abnormal wall motion, most often the apex after an anterior infarction. It should be seen in two views; with an enhancing agent it shows as a filling defect.
3. A papillary fibroelastoma is a small mass on the downstream side of a valve, most often the aortic side of the aortic valve. Its mobility predicts embolism.

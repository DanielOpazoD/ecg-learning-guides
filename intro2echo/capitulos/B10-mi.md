## Complications of myocardial infarction

Septal rupture, papillary muscle rupture, free-wall rupture and LV aneurysm

An infarcted wall can tear, through the septum, a papillary muscle or the free wall, or it can stretch into an aneurysm. Echocardiography shows each lesion and measures its effect on flow and pressure.

**Ventricular septal rupture with a significant shunt.** An 11-mm defect in the akinetic apical septum carries left-to-right flow in systole[159](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-159). Qp/Qs is (2.3² × 27) ÷ (2.0² × 18) = 2.0, 1.5 or more: a hemodynamically significant shunt[162](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-162). CW Doppler across the rupture peaks at 3.8 m/s, an LV–RV gradient of 58 mmHg. With a systolic blood pressure of 103 mmHg, the RV systolic pressure is 103 − 58 = 45 mmHg (mildly raised)[160](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-160), [32](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-32). The RV takes a volume load[161](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-161).

### Mechanical complications

A transmural infarction can rupture the septum, a papillary muscle or the free wall, typically 3 to 5 days after the infarction. Most of these mechanical complications are surgical emergencies, and echocardiography shows each of them[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). A scar that thins forms an aneurysm[158](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-158). Each lesion lies in the territory of the infarct ( [chapter 10](https://intro2echo.com/segments)).

### Ventricular septal rupture

A septal rupture occurs in fewer than 0.3% of patients in the era of routine primary revascularization, and without repair the mortality approaches 80% at 30 days[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). Anterior and apical ruptures follow infarcts in the LAD territory, and posterior ruptures follow inferior infarcts, which are associated with complex ruptures with multiple, irregular connections[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157).

Color Doppler shows the left-to-right flow across the septum: it found all 12 confirmed ruptures, and two-dimensional criteria found 7[159](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-159). The peak velocity of the jet on CW gives the LV–RV gradient as 4V², and the systolic blood pressure minus that gradient is the RV systolic pressure[160](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-160). [Bonus chapter B8](https://intro2echo.com/shunts) applies the same relation to a congenital VSD and measures Qp/Qs from the two outflow tracts.

The shunt overloads the RV with volume, raises the pulmonary blood flow and, in turn, overloads the LA and the LV[161](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-161). A shunt is hemodynamically significant when Qp/Qs is 1.5 or more, or when a chamber downstream of it enlarges[162](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-162); after a septal rupture, Qp/Qs reaches 8:1 at catheterization, depending on the size of the defect[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). In the figure the rupture grows to 18 mm; in one series the rupture measured 13.9 ± 4.4 mm by echocardiography[163](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-163).

### Papillary muscle rupture

Papillary muscle rupture complicates 0.05% to 0.26% of acute infarctions in the reperfusion era. The anterolateral muscle has a dual blood supply; the posteromedial muscle has a single supply, from the circumflex or the right coronary artery. So anterolateral rupture is extremely uncommon, and posteromedial rupture typically follows an inferior or lateral infarction[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). The posteromedial muscle most commonly has two heads[164](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-164), and the rupture may be partial or complete[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157).

The posteromedial muscle holds the medial half of both leaflets[165](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-165). Its rupture can disrupt either leaflet[166](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-166). A flail leaflet has lost its attachment to the LV, and its tip points toward the roof of the LA[165](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-165); the torn head is a mobile mass in the LV that sometimes prolapses into the LA[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). In operated patients, transthoracic echocardiography made the diagnosis in 53.8%, and a flail leaflet was seen in every complete rupture and in 40% of partial ones[167](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-167); when the transthoracic study is not diagnostic, TEE has a high sensitivity[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157).

A flail leaflet or a ruptured papillary muscle is specific for severe MR. In acute severe MR, a low blood pressure and a high LA pressure lower the driving pressure, so the jet is slow and color may show little turbulence. The jet is often eccentric, and an eccentric jet looks smaller than a central jet of the same severity. A hyperdynamic LV with a low forward flow supports the diagnosis, the LV and the LA can be of normal size, and the pulmonary veins usually show systolic flow reversal. The Doppler grades of chronic MR ( [chapter 15](https://intro2echo.com/mr)) do not apply[29](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-29).

A triangular CW contour with an early peak shows a high LA pressure or a large v wave, but it is not sensitive for severe MR[29](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-29). The systolic blood pressure minus the peak MR gradient estimates the LA pressure[168](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-168).

### Free-wall rupture and pseudoaneurysm

Free-wall rupture usually causes sudden death. It presents with tamponade and shock, and echocardiography shows the tamponade and sometimes flow across the defect[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). [Bonus chapter B5](https://intro2echo.com/peri) grades the effusion and the signs of tamponade.

When adherent pericardium contains the rupture, a pseudoaneurysm forms; a true aneurysm, in contrast, is a thinned scar[158](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-158). Pseudoaneurysms more often involve the inferior or the lateral wall and usually have a narrow neck[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). In pseudoaneurysms the width of the neck was at most 0.5 of the largest parallel width of the sac, and the sac extended behind the intact wall; in true aneurysms the ratio was 0.9 to 1.0[169](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-169).

The wall of the sac is thrombus or pericardium without myocardium. Red flags on imaging include a narrow neck and a break in the myocardium. Doppler shows turbulent flow across the neck, and an enhancing agent helps to show the sac; in published cases, echocardiography missed 18.6% of the pseudoaneurysms[170](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-170). A pseudoaneurysm is prone to rupture[171](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-171), and it is a surgical emergency[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). An effusion that holds clot is hard to assess by TTE; TEE, CT or CMR assess it better[128](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-128).

### True aneurysm and thrombus

An aneurysm is a focal dilatation with thinning and an akinetic or dyskinetic wall; the ASE scores it as akinetic or dyskinetic, with no separate score[14](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-14). Its wall is thin scar, most often at the anterior wall or the apex after a total occlusion of the LAD, and it is associated with angina, thrombus, worsening heart failure and ventricular tachyarrhythmia[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). The mouth of a true aneurysm is wide, nearly as wide as the aneurysm itself[169](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-169).

A thrombus lies in up to 50% of chronic LV aneurysms, and it embolizes less often there, probably because the aneurysm does not contract[44](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-44). [Bonus chapter B9](https://intro2echo.com/masses) shows the types of LV thrombus and the enhancing agent.

### RV infarction

The more proximal a right coronary occlusion, the more RV myocardium the infarct involves[33](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-33), and RV-predominant shock follows a right coronary infarct that involves the RV[157](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-157). McConnell's sign occurs in RV infarction as often as in pulmonary embolism[172](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-172), and S′ assumes that one segment represents the whole RV, which is not likely in a regional disease such as RV infarction[33](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-33). [Chapter 11](https://intro2echo.com/rv) measures the size and the function of the RV.

### Key points

1. Color Doppler shows a septal rupture's left-to-right flow; the RV systolic pressure is the systolic blood pressure minus 4V² of the jet.
2. Papillary muscle rupture usually involves the posteromedial muscle, after an inferior or lateral infarction. The flail leaflet makes the MR severe, even when the jet looks small and slow.
3. A free-wall rupture bleeds into the pericardium and causes tamponade. A contained rupture forms a pseudoaneurysm, with a neck at most 0.5 of the sac's width.
4. A true aneurysm has a wide mouth and a wall of thin scar; up to 50% of chronic aneurysms hold a thrombus.

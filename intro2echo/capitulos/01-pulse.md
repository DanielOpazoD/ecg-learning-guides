## Pulse

The pulse-echo principle

Each scan line of an echocardiographic image is formed from a short ultrasound pulse and the echoes that return from tissue boundaries. The ultrasound system measures the arrival time of each echo and converts it to depth.

At 2.0 MHz the wavelength is 0.77 mm. A 2-cycle pulse has a spatial pulse length of 1.54 mm, which gives an axial resolution of **0.77 mm**. The echo from the posterior wall at 10.3 cm returns after 134 µs and is attenuated by 21 dB over the round trip.

### Echo arrival time and depth

The ultrasound system assumes that sound travels at 1540 m/s in all soft tissue[1](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-1). Depth is calculated as speed × time ÷ 2, because the pulse travels to the reflector and back[1](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-1). The round trip takes approximately 13 µs for each centimeter of depth, so the echo from a reflector at 10 cm arrives after approximately 130 µs[2](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-2).

### Wavelength and axial resolution

wavelength = speed ÷ frequency  ·  axial resolution = cycles × wavelength ÷ 2

At 7 MHz the wavelength is approximately 0.22 mm[3](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-3). Axial resolution equals half the spatial pulse length, because the echo travels a round trip[1](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-1). A higher transmit frequency or fewer cycles per pulse shorten the pulse, so that two reflectors that lie closer together along the beam are displayed as two separate echoes.

### Acoustic impedance and reflection

Acoustic impedance is the product of tissue density and the speed of sound[1](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-1). At each boundary the reflected fraction of the incident intensity is ((Z2 − Z1) ÷ (Z2 + Z1))². A fat–muscle boundary reflects approximately 1.4% of the intensity[3](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-3). A boundary between soft tissue and air reflects almost all of the intensity, so the lung blocks the beam and no image is formed beyond it.

### Attenuation and penetration depth

Absorption and scattering in tissue attenuate the pulse, and the attenuation increases approximately in proportion to frequency[1](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-1). The textbook average attenuation coefficient for soft tissue is 0.5 dB per cm per MHz; attenuation is much lower in blood (0.17 dB/cm at 1 MHz) and higher in muscle (1.2 dB/cm)[1](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-1). In the figure, the attenuation is summed layer by layer along the beam.

- Adult transthoracic studies use transmit frequencies of 2.0 to 5.0 MHz[4](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-4).
- The ASE recommends the highest transmit frequency that still penetrates to the depth of interest[4](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-4).
- Time gain compensation amplifies the late echoes from deep structures to offset attenuation[4](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-4). It also amplifies the noise.

### Key points

1. Depth is calculated from the echo arrival time; the round trip takes approximately 13 µs per centimeter of depth.
2. A higher transmit frequency improves axial resolution and reduces the depth of penetration.
3. Large differences in acoustic impedance at a boundary produce bright echoes. Air and bone block the ultrasound beam.

## Cuestionario del original

### 1. A reflector lies at a depth of 8 cm. When does its echo arrive after the pulse is transmitted?

1. After approximately 52 µs
2. After approximately 104 µs
3. After approximately 208 µs

**Respuesta del original:** After approximately 104 µs

**Explicación:** The round trip takes approximately 13 µs per centimeter of depth, so the echo arrives after 8 × 13 ≈ 104 µs.

### 2. The transmit frequency increases from 2.5 MHz to 5 MHz with 2 cycles per pulse. What happens to axial resolution?

1. It halves (finer detail)
2. It doubles (coarser detail)
3. It does not change

**Respuesta del original:** It halves (finer detail)

**Explicación:** The wavelength halves, so the spatial pulse length and the axial resolution halve. The depth of penetration decreases.

### 3. Why does the lung block the parasternal ultrasound beam?

1. Air absorbs the ultrasound
2. The tissue–air impedance difference causes almost total reflection
3. The pulse is too short

**Respuesta del original:** The tissue–air impedance difference causes almost total reflection

**Explicación:** The acoustic impedance of air is far lower than that of soft tissue, so nearly all of the incident intensity is reflected at the tissue–air boundary.

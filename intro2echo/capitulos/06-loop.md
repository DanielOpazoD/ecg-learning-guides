## Loop

LV pressure–volume analysis

The LV pressure–volume loop plots LV pressure against LV volume over one cardiac cycle. Changes in contractility, afterload and chamber stiffness alter the position and shape of the loop and change the ejection fraction.

**Normal.** At an end-diastolic pressure of 9.0 mmHg, the ventricle fills to an end-diastolic volume of 120 mL. It ejects a stroke volume of 72 mL against an end-systolic pressure of 116 mmHg, with an EF of 60% and an Ea/Ees ratio of 0.67[21](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-21). The stroke work, equal to the area of the loop, is 1.04 J.

### Phases of the loop

The loop is traversed counterclockwise through the four phases of the cardiac cycle ( [chapter 05](https://intro2echo.com/cycle)): isovolumic contraction, ejection, isovolumic relaxation and filling[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18). The width of the loop is the stroke volume, and its area is the stroke work[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18).

### ESPVR, EDPVR and arterial elastance

- **ESPVR.** The end-systolic pressure–volume points lie on a nearly straight line, P = Ees × (V − V0)[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18). Its slope, Ees, is a relatively load-independent measure of contractility[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18), [19](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-19).
- **EDPVR.** The end-diastolic pressure–volume relation is curvilinear and close to exponential, and it describes the passive stiffness of the chamber[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18).
- **Ea.** Effective arterial elastance is end-systolic pressure ÷ stroke volume, which equals the absolute value of the slope of the line from the EDV on the volume axis to the end-systolic point[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18), [20](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-20).

### Ventricular–arterial coupling and ejection fraction

EF = Ees ÷ (Ees + Ea)  (with V0 taken as 0)

EF depends on both the ventricle and its load and is therefore not a pure measure of contractility[21](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-21). An increase in afterload decreases EF even when contractility is unchanged. Stroke work is maximal when Ea equals Ees, and efficiency is highest at a lower Ea/Ees[22](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-22). In healthy adults at rest, Ea/Ees is about 0.5 to 1.0, depending on the method of measurement[18](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-18), [23](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-23).

Without invasive pressure measurement, end-systolic pressure is often estimated as 0.9 × the systolic cuff pressure[24](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-24).

### Preset conditions

- **Hypocontractile ventricle (HFrEF).** With a low Ees, the ventricle dilates and ejects a small fraction of its end-diastolic volume.
- **Stiff ventricle (HFpEF).** With a steep EDPVR, filling pressure is high at a normal volume, and EF remains normal.
- **Pressure load.** With a high Ea, as in aortic stenosis or hypertension, the loop rises to a high systolic pressure.
- **Volume load (MR).** This preset reproduces the loop of the Barlow MR heart, with chronic severe primary mitral regurgitation ( [chapter 15](https://intro2echo.com/mr)). The loop is wide and shifted to the right; EDV is much larger, and EDP is only slightly higher[25](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-25). The LV ejects into the LA from the onset of contraction, so there is no isovolumic contraction, and the mitral valve opens early[25](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-25), [26](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-26). Only part of the stroke volume is ejected forward into the aorta[26](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-26).

Preload is the stretch of the myocardium before contraction, and afterload is the load against which the ventricle ejects[27](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-27), [28](https://intro2echo.com/#ref-28).

### Key points

1. Ees, the slope of the ESPVR, is an index of contractility.
2. Effective arterial elastance is calculated as Ea = end-systolic pressure ÷ stroke volume.
3. EF depends on both Ees and Ea, as EF = Ees ÷ (Ees + Ea).
4. In chronic severe mitral regurgitation, the loop has no isovolumic contraction phase and is wide and shifted to the right.

## Cuestionario del original

### 1. Afterload (Ea) increases while contractility (Ees) is unchanged. How does the EF change?

1. It decreases
2. It increases
3. It does not change

**Respuesta del original:** It decreases

**Explicación:** Because EF = Ees ÷ (Ees + Ea), a larger Ea at the same Ees gives a lower EF.

### 2. The slope of which relation is an index of contractility?

1. The EDPVR
2. The ESPVR
3. The Ea line

**Respuesta del original:** The ESPVR

**Explicación:** The slope of the end-systolic pressure–volume relation, Ees, is a relatively load-independent index of contractility.

### 3. In a ventricle with increased chamber stiffness, which change appears first on the pressure–volume loop?

1. A steeper EDPVR, with a higher filling pressure at the same volume
2. A decrease in the ESPVR slope
3. A fall in Ea to zero

**Respuesta del original:** A steeper EDPVR, with a higher filling pressure at the same volume

**Explicación:** Chamber stiffness is a passive property of the ventricle, so it changes the end-diastolic pressure–volume relation.

### 4. Which phase of the pressure–volume loop is absent in chronic severe mitral regurgitation?

1. Isovolumic contraction
2. Ejection
3. Filling

**Respuesta del original:** Isovolumic contraction

**Explicación:** The LV ejects into the LA from the onset of contraction, so LV volume decreases before the aortic valve opens.
